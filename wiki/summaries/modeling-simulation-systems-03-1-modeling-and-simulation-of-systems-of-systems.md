## Key Points

- Frames "virtual build and test" as the only workable alternative to dangerous, costly, unethical, or risky real-world SoS experimentation.
- Five motivating SoS examples each follow the same template (objective, conventional limitation, SoS approach, virtual build and test): national healthcare cost coordination, cloud system architectures, climate-resilient food crops, catastrophic wildfires, and lagging drug development.
- DEVS is positioned as a foundational formalism — analogous to arithmetic underlying calculation — for simulating discrete-event SoS models on a common time base, even with heterogeneous internal formalisms.
- SES is introduced as a high-level ontology framework for hierarchical/modular composition of large complex systems, governed by a small set of axioms.
- The same DEVS models can move from simulation to real-time distributed operation via data-distribution middleware (e.g., Web services), reducing duplicated effort across the system lifecycle.
- SoS often spans multiple disciplines and formalisms; DEVS serves as a universal computational basis that accommodates limitless domain-specific formalisms.
- Three featured DEVS environments: MS4 Me (commercial, FDDEVS-based, beginner-friendly), CoSMoS/DEVS-Suite (academic, hierarchical parallel DEVS with database-backed model storage), and VLE (Virtual Laboratory Environment, adopted by INRA for living-systems SoS).
- The book traces back to Zeigler's 1976 "Theory of Modeling and Simulation" and a literature lineage including books by Zeigler, Praehofer, Kim, Sarjoughian, Cellier, Wainer, Mosterman, Mittal, Risco-Martín, Yilmaz, Ören, and Nutaro.
- Target audience: first/second-year graduate students, practitioners (simulation devs, systems engineers, managers), and academics.
- Chapters 2–8 cover DEVS basics in MS4 Me; 9–12 advanced; 13–18 applications across the three environments.
