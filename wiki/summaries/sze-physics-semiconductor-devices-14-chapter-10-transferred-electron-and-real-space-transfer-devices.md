## Key Points

- The transferred-electron device (TED, also called the Gunn diode) generates microwave oscillations by exploiting the intervalley electron transfer in semiconductors like GaAs, InP, and GaN, where a low-mobility, higher-energy satellite valley exists ~0.3-0.5 eV above the main Gamma valley.
- Transferred-electron effect: as the local field rises, electrons gain enough energy to transfer from the high-mobility (low-mass) Gamma valley to the low-mobility (high-mass) L or X satellite valleys; the population shift produces a region of negative differential mobility (NDM) and hence negative differential resistance.
- Domain formation: in a uniformly biased TED, a high-field domain nucleates near the cathode and travels to the anode at the saturation velocity; once it arrives, the bias re-distributes and a new domain forms. This produces a self-sustained oscillation at the transit-time frequency f = v_s / L.
- Modes of operation: transit-time, delayed-domain (LSA), quenched-domain, and stable-amplifier modes; LSA mode operates above the transit-time frequency by stopping domain formation through fast bias swing.
- Device performance: GaAs and InP Gunn diodes generate up to a few watts CW at the X and Ka bands; GaN TEDs extend operation to higher power and temperature.
- Real-space-transfer (RST) devices: instead of transferring electrons between valleys in k-space, RST devices transfer them between layers of different bandgap (a narrow-gap channel and a wide-gap barrier layer) in real space; analogous to TED but operating in heterojunctions. Both diode (two-terminal NDR) and transistor (three-terminal, with a separate collector layer) versions exist.
- RST devices offer faster intervalley transfer (set by ballistic transit across the heterojunction barrier rather than scattering-limited intervalley transfer) and potentially higher operating frequencies.
