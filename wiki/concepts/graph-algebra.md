## Definition

A graph algebra describes a class of graphs by operations that build larger graphs from smaller ones. The Kloks-Xiao text distinguishes two flavors:
- Gluing: a larger graph is formed from G_1 and G_2 by identifying certain vertices. Chordal graphs are gluing-defined (chordal-built via simplicial / clique-tree composition).
- Bridging: a larger graph is formed by adding edges between certain subsets of vertices of G_1 and G_2. Trees, cographs (join/union), and distance-hereditary graphs are bridging-defined.

## How It Works

Each algebraic operation corresponds to a node label in the decomposition tree of the graph. For cographs the algebra has two operations (⊕ for disjoint union, ⊗ for join), giving the cotree. For distance-hereditary graphs the operations include pendant addition and twin addition.

Graph algebras pair naturally with monadic second-order logic: the class of graphs generated by an algebra of bounded "tree-width-like" complexity admits Courcelle-style meta-theorems.

## Key Parameters

- Set of generators (atomic graphs).
- Set of operations and their arities.

## When To Use

- For defining graph classes recursively.
- As a structural foundation for FPT algorithms via decomposition trees.

## Risks & Pitfalls

- Confusing algebraic definitions with structural characterizations (e.g. P_4-free for cographs vs. union/join recursion).
- Some classes (like AT-free graphs) lack clean algebraic descriptions.
